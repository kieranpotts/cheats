= AWS: Pricing models

AWS pricing is based on three things:

1. *Compute*: The amount of resources used, such as CPU and RAM, by the duration
   of time (normally in minutes) the resources are in use.

2. *Storage*: The quantity of data stored or allocated.

3. *Outbound data transfer*: The quantity of data that is transferred _out_ from
   an availability zone or region.

For storage, you are normally paid for _utilization_. This is the case for S3,
for example. But in some cases the price is based on storage _allocation_. This
is the case for EBS volumes, for example. So, when you launch an EC2 instance,
you will choose an instance type which determines the amount of CPU, RAM, and
storage that is allocated to the instance. If you shut down the instance, you
will no longer be billed for CPU and RAM time, but you will continue to be
billed for the associated EBS storage.

Typically, data that goes _in_ to AWS is not charged. So, for S3 you pay for the
storage and for getting data out, but not for uploading data to buckets in the
first place.

== Snowball pricing

The Snowball family (see the migration and transfer services page) is billed
differently from most AWS services — you pay a flat *service fee per job*
(varying by device size), plus the cost of *shipping* the device. Up to 10 days
of onsite usage of the device is included free with each job; extra days are
charged daily. Transferring data _onto_ AWS via Snowball (inbound transfer) is
free.

== Always-free services

A handful of services never contribute to your bill, regardless of usage,
because they're either free by design or their cost is folded into other
billed resources: *VPC*, *IAM*, *AWS Organizations* / Consolidated Billing,
*AWS CloudFormation*, *Auto Scaling*, and *Elastic Beanstalk* itself (though the
EC2/ELB/etc. resources it provisions on your behalf are billed normally). Worth
remembering when reasoning about where costs actually originate in an
architecture.
