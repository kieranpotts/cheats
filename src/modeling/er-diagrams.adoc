= Entity-Relationship Diagrams

An entity-relationship (ER) model describes interrelated things of interest in
a specific domain of knowledge. It is composed of *entity types* (which
classify the things of interest) and specifies *relationships* that can exist
between entities (instances of those entity types).

ER modeling was developed for database design by
https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_Chen[Peter Chen] and published in a 1976
paper. It is commonly used for teaching the basics of database structure and
for designing relational databases.

ER models are typically built at three levels of abstraction:

* **Conceptual data model** — the highest level; establishes the overall scope
  and defines master reference data entities. Used to document data
  architecture.
* **Logical data model** — adds operational and transactional data entities
  with full details, independent of any specific database management system.
* **Physical data model** — contains enough detail to instantiate a database,
  including tables, indexes, and constraints. Technology-dependent.

== Components

=== Entities

An *entity* is a thing that exists either physically or logically — a physical
object (house, car), an event (house sale, car service), or a concept (customer
transaction, order). An *entity type* (or entity set) is a category; an
*entity* is an instance of a given entity type.

Entities can be thought of as *nouns*: a computer, an employee, a song, a
mathematical theorem.

All entities except weak entities must have a minimal set of uniquely
identifying attributes that may be used as a primary key.

A *weak entity* has no attributes of its own that are sufficient to uniquely
identify its instances. Instead, it is existence-dependent on another entity
— its *owner* (or *identifying entity*) — and is identified by combining its
own *partial key* (or *discriminator*) with the primary key of its owner. For
example, a `Room` entity might be weak with respect to `Building`: room
numbers are only unique within a building, so a `Room` is identified by
`(BuildingID, RoomNumber)`, not `RoomNumber` alone. The relationship that
connects a weak entity to its owner is called an *identifying relationship*.

=== Attributes

Entities and relationships can both have *attributes* — properties that
describe them. For example, an `Employee` entity might have a `SSN` attribute,
while a `Proved` relationship may have a `Date` attribute.

Attributes are often omitted from diagrams to avoid clutter.

=== Relationships

A *relationship* captures how entities are related to one another.
Relationships can be thought of as *verbs* linking two or more nouns:
an `Owns` relationship between a `Company` and a `Computer`, a `Supervises`
relationship between an `Employee` and a `Department`.

A *relationship set* is the collection of all relationships of the same type
— analogous to a relation in mathematics.

== Chen's notation

Peter Chen's original notation uses:

* **Rectangles** for entity sets
* **Diamonds** for relationships
* **Ovals** for attributes, connected by a line to exactly one entity or
  relationship set
* **Lines** connecting entities to relationships

Cardinality constraints in Chen's notation:

[cols="1,3"]
|===
| Notation | Meaning

| Double line
| Total participation — all entities in the entity set must participate in at
  least one relationship in the relationship set

| Arrow from entity to relationship
| Key constraint — each entity can participate in at most one relationship
  (injectivity)

| Thick line (double line + arrow)
| Bijectivity — each entity is involved in exactly one relationship

| Underlined attribute name
| Key — two different entities or relationships with this attribute always
  have different values
|===

A weak entity is drawn as a double-bordered rectangle, and its identifying
relationship as a double-bordered diamond. Its partial key attribute is
underlined with a dashed line, rather than the solid underline used for a
full primary key.

PlantUML has no native support for Chen's diamond notation, but a rough
approximation (entities and relationships as boxes, connected by lines) can
be built as follows:

[plantuml]
....
rectangle "Building" as building
rectangle "Room" as room <<weak entity>>
diamond "Has" as has

building -- has
has -- room : "1" ..> "*"
....

Chen's notation also handles relationships among three or more entities
directly — the diamond simply connects to every participating entity. For
example, a ternary `Supplies` relationship might connect `Supplier`, `Part`,
and `Project` entities, capturing facts like "Supplier S supplies Part P to
Project J" that cannot be decomposed into three independent binary
relationships without losing information.

== Crow's foot notation

Crow's foot notation is a family of related ER notations, the most widely
used for ER diagrams in industry. It represents entities as boxes and
relationships as lines between them, with different shapes at the ends of
each line representing cardinality. The best-known member of the family is
https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Data_modeling#Barker's_notation[Barker's
notation] (Richard Barker's Oracle CASE Method), described below; other
variants (such as Information Engineering notation) differ in minor
graphical details, such as how "optional" is marked.

Three basic symbols:

[cols="1,3"]
|===
| Symbol | Meaning

| Ring (`O`)
| Zero

| Dash (`|`)
| One

| Crow's foot (`--<`)
| Many (infinite)
|===

These symbols are used in pairs to represent cardinality. The *inner*
component represents the minimum, the *outer* component represents the
maximum:

[cols="1,3"]
|===
| Notation | Meaning

| `O` + `|`
| Minimum zero, maximum one (optional)

| `|` + `|`
| Minimum one, maximum one (mandatory)

| `O` + `--<`
| Minimum zero, maximum many (optional)

| `|` + `--<`
| Minimum one, maximum many (mandatory)
|===

In crow's foot notation, relationships cannot have attributes. Where
necessary, relationships are promoted to entities in their own right. For
example, if it is necessary to capture where and when an `Artist` performed a
`Song`, a new entity `Performance` is introduced (with attributes for time and
place), and the relationship becomes indirect.

Unlike Chen's notation, attributes are not drawn as separate ovals — they are
listed directly inside the entity box, primary keys marked (commonly `PK`)
and foreign keys marked (commonly `FK`).

[plantuml]
....
entity "Customer" as customer {
  * id : int <<PK>>
  --
  name : varchar
  email : varchar
}

entity "Order" as order {
  * id : int <<PK>>
  --
  customer_id : int <<FK>>
  placed_at : datetime
}

customer ||--o{ order : places
....

This renders the mandatory-one-to-optional-many cardinality of "a customer
places zero or more orders; every order belongs to exactly one customer."

== Enhanced entity-relationship (EER) model

The enhanced entity-relationship (EER) model is an extended version of the ER
model developed in the 1980s by database researchers including Ramez Elmasri
and Shamkant Navathe. It incorporates additional semantic constructs for
modeling complex databases in domains such as engineering design,
manufacturing systems, telecommunications, GIS, and complex software
applications.

The EER model retains all concepts from the basic ER model — entities,
attributes, relationships, keys, and cardinality constraints — while adding
support for:

* Subclass and superclass relationships (classification hierarchies)
* Specialization and generalization (top-down and bottom-up abstraction)
* Attribute and relationship inheritance
* Union types (categories)
* Constraints on hierarchies (disjointness, completeness)

=== Subclass and superclass

A *subclass* (or subtype) is an entity type that has distinct attributes or
relationships in addition to those inherited from another entity type, called
the *superclass* (or supertype). The subclass is a subset of the superclass
— every instance of a subclass is also an instance of its superclass.

For example, in a university database, `Person` might be a superclass with
subclasses `Student`, `Professor`, and `Staff`. Each subclass inherits common
attributes like `Name`, `Address`, and `DateOfBirth` from `Person`, while
having specific attributes such as `StudentID` and `Major` for `Student`, or
`Rank` and `Department` for `Professor`.

Formally, if `S` is a subclass of `C` (the superclass):

* `S ⊆ C` — every member of `S` is also a member of `C`
* `S` inherits all attributes and relationships of `C`
* `S` may have additional specific attributes and relationships

=== Specialization

*Specialization* is a top-down conceptual refinement process where a
higher-level entity type (superclass) is subdivided into lower-level, more
specialized entity types (subclasses) based on distinguishing characteristics.
It emphasizes differences among entities within the superclass.

Process:

1. Start with a general entity type.
2. Identify distinguishing characteristics.
3. Define subclasses based on these characteristics.
4. Assign specific attributes and relationships to each subclass.

For example, starting with an `Employee` entity, specialization might identify
distinct types based on job function:

* `Employee` (superclass) specializes into:
  * `Secretary` (with attribute `TypingSpeed`)
  * `Engineer` (with attribute `EngineeringType`)
  * `Technician` (with attribute `TechGrade`)

Specialization can be:

* **Attribute-defined** — based on a specific attribute value (e.g.,
  `EmployeeType`).
* **User-defined** — not based on any specific attribute condition.

=== Generalization

*Generalization* is a bottom-up abstraction process where multiple entity
types with common characteristics are combined into a higher-level,
generalized entity type (superclass). It emphasizes similarities among
entities.

Process:

1. Identify entity types with common attributes.
2. Extract common attributes into a new superclass.
3. Establish subclass relationships.
4. Retain specialized attributes in subclasses.

For example, separate entities `Car`, `Truck`, and `Motorcycle` can be
generalized into a superclass `Vehicle` containing common attributes like
`VehicleID`, `Color`, `Year`, and `Manufacturer`, while specific attributes
remain with subclasses.

Specialization and generalization are inverse processes: specialization
creates subclasses from a superclass, while generalization creates a
superclass from existing entity types.

=== Attribute and relationship inheritance

*Inheritance* is a fundamental principle of the EER model where subclasses
automatically inherit all attributes and relationships from their
superclass(es). This promotes data consistency and reduces redundancy.

Types of inheritance:

* **Single inheritance** — a subclass inherits from exactly one superclass
  (e.g., `Student` inherits from `Person`).
* **Multiple inheritance** — a subclass inherits from multiple superclasses,
  acquiring attributes and relationships from all parents (e.g.,
  `TeachingAssistant` might inherit from both `Student` and `Employee`).

Inheritance properties:

* **Transitive** — if `C` is a subclass of `B`, and `B` is a subclass of `A`,
  then `C` inherits from both `B` and `A`.
* **Additive** — subclasses can define additional attributes and relationships
  beyond inherited ones.
* **Overriding** — generally not supported in EER (unlike object-oriented
  models).

=== Union types (categories)

A *union type* (or *category*) represents a subclass that is a subset of the
*union* of two or more distinct superclasses. Unlike regular subclasses that
inherit from a single superclass, a category can have instances from multiple
different entity types.

Characteristics:

* An instance of a category must belong to exactly one of the superclasses.
* The category inherits attributes common to all superclasses.
* Each instance maintains its identity in the originating superclass.

**Notation**: Represented by a circle with the union symbol (∪) connected to
all superclasses, with an arc and subset symbol (⊂) connecting to the
category.

For example, in a vehicle registration database, an `Owner` category can be
defined as a subset of `PERSON ∪ BANK ∪ COMPANY`. Each vehicle owner is
either a person, a bank, or a company, but not multiple types simultaneously.
The `Owner` category would have a relationship `Owns` with the `Vehicle`
entity.

Formally, if category `T` is defined on superclasses `S1, S2, ..., Sn`:

* `T ⊆ S1 ∪ S2 ∪ ... ∪ Sn`
* For each instance `t ∈ T`, `t ∈ Si` for exactly one `i`

=== Aggregation

*Aggregation* is an abstraction concept that treats a relationship as a
higher-level entity, allowing relationships to participate in other
relationships. This is useful when modeling complex scenarios where
relationships themselves have attributes or participate in additional
relationships.

For example, in a project management system:

* Employees work on Projects (relationship: `WorksOn`).
* For each work assignment, specific Machinery is required.
* The aggregation treats the entire `WorksOn` relationship as an entity that
  can be related to `Machinery`.

This allows modeling "Employee `E` working on Project `P` requires Machinery
`M`" without creating artificial intermediate entities.

=== EER notation summary

EER notation adds the following to standard ER notation:

[cols="1,3"]
|===
| Notation | Meaning

| Triangle connecting supertype to subtypes
| Generalization/specialization hierarchy (similar to UML generalization)

| `d` in a circle
| Disjoint subtypes — an instance belongs to at most one subtype

| `o` in a circle
| Overlapping subtypes — an instance can belong to multiple subtypes

| Circle with ∪ symbol
| Union type (category) — subset of the union of multiple superclasses

| Double rectangle
| Weak entity (same as basic ER)

| Dashed line connecting category to superclasses
| Category membership
|===

== Model usability issues

=== Fan trap

Occurs when a master table links to multiple tables in a one-to-many
relationship, creating a visual "fan" pattern. When calculating sums over
aggregates using standard SQL, the results can be incorrect due to
double-counting. This is common in star schema designs used in data
warehouses.

*Mitigation*: Adjust the data model or the SQL query to account for the fan
structure.

=== Chasm trap

Occurs when a model suggests the existence of a relationship between entity
types, but the pathway between them is incomplete or missing in certain
instances. For example, a `Building` has one or more `Rooms`, and `Rooms`
hold zero or more `Computers`. A query for all `Computers` in a `Building`
would miss `Computers` not currently assigned to a `Room`.

*Mitigation*: Add a direct relationship between the entities that need to be
queried together.

== ER diagrams vs. UML class diagrams

ER diagrams and UML class diagrams serve different purposes:

[cols="1,3"]
|===
| Aspect | ER diagrams | UML class diagrams

| Primary use
| Database design and data modeling
| Object-oriented software design

| Origin
| Peter Chen (1976)
| Grady Booch, Ivar Jacobson, James Rumbaugh (1990s)

| Entities
| Entity types (rectangles)
| Classes (three-compartment rectangles)

| Relationships
| Lines with cardinality notation
| Associations, aggregations, compositions, generalizations

| Attributes
| Ovals (Chen) or listed in entity boxes (crow's foot)
| Listed in the middle compartment

| Methods
| Not represented
| Listed in the bottom compartment

| Focus
| Data structure and constraints
| Behavior and object interactions
|===

== Guidelines

* Use nouns for entity type names (e.g., `Customer`, `Order`, `Product`).
* Use verbs or verb phrases for relationship names (e.g., `Places`, `Contains`).
* Keep diagrams simple — avoid clutter by omitting attributes where possible.
* Crow's foot notation is more common in industry tooling; Chen's notation is
  more common in academic and teaching contexts.
* Model at the appropriate level of abstraction (conceptual, logical, or
  physical).
* Identify and resolve fan traps and chasm traps early in the design process.
* Use EER modeling when generalization/specialization hierarchies are needed.
* An ER model does not show an entity's life history — use state diagrams or
  process models for that.
* An ER diagram captures structure, not the level of redundancy in that
  structure — normalize the resulting schema (1NF, 2NF, 3NF, and beyond as
  needed) as a separate, follow-on step.
