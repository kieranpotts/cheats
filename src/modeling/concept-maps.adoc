= Concept Maps

A *concept map* (or conceptual diagram) is a diagram that depicts suggested
relationships between concepts. It typically represents ideas and information
as boxes or circles (nodes), connected with labeled arrows (links) that
describe the relationship between concepts — often in a downward-branching
hierarchical structure but also in free-form maps.

Concept mapping was developed by education professor
https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_D._Novak[Joseph D. Novak] and his
research team at Cornell University in the 1970s, based on the cognitive
theories of https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Ausubel[David Ausubel].
It is grounded in the learning movement called
https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constructivism_(learning_theory)[constructivism].

== Components

=== Concepts

A *concept* is a perceived regularity in events or objects, or a record of
events or objects, designated by a label. Concepts are typically enclosed in
boxes or circles (nodes).

Examples: `Gravity`, `Photosynthesis`, `Customer`, `Database`.

=== Linking phrases

A *linking phrase* (or linking word) is a word or short phrase written on a
line (arrow) that connects two concepts, describing the relationship between
them. The combination of two concepts and a linking phrase forms a
*proposition* — a meaningful statement.

Examples of linking phrases: `causes`, `requires`, `such as`, `contributes
to`, `is a type of`, `leads to`, `depends on`.

=== Propositions

A *proposition* is a statement about some object or event in the universe,
formed by two or more concepts connected by linking words. Propositions are the
fundamental unit of meaning in a concept map.

Example: `Gravity` --(causes)--> `Objects to fall` forms the proposition
"Gravity causes objects to fall."

=== Cross-links

A *cross-link* is a relationship between concepts in different segments or
domains of the concept map. Cross-links help illustrate how knowledge in one
area relates to knowledge in another, and are often a sign of deeper
understanding.

== Structure

Concept maps typically follow a hierarchical structure:

* The most general, inclusive concepts are placed at the *top*.
* More specific, less general concepts are arranged *below*.
* The map is read from top to bottom.

However, unlike mind maps (which radiate from a single central concept),
concept maps can have *multiple hubs and clusters*, forming a network rather
than a strict tree.

== How to construct a concept map

1. **Identify a focus question** — a clear question that the concept map will
   help answer (e.g., "What is water?", "What causes the seasons?").
2. **Brainstorm concepts** — list 15-25 key concepts relevant to the focus
   question.
3. **Rank concepts** — order them from most general/inclusive to most
   specific.
4. **Build the map** — place the most general concept at the top, then add
   more specific concepts below, connecting them with linking phrases.
5. **Add cross-links** — look for relationships between concepts in different
   parts of the map.
6. **Revise** — refine the map as understanding deepens.

== Uses

Concept maps are widely used in education and business for:

* **Note taking and summarizing** — gleaning key concepts and their
  relationships from documents and source materials.
* **Knowledge creation** — transforming tacit knowledge into an organizational
  resource.
* **Knowledge preservation** — eliciting and mapping expert knowledge before
  employee retirement.
* **Collaborative knowledge modeling** — creating shared understanding within
  a team.
* **Instructional design** — serving as "advance organizers" that provide an
  initial conceptual frame for learning.
* **Training** — representing the context and relationship of training to
  organizational goals.
* **Communicating complex ideas** — exposing the structure of an argument or
  train of thought.
* **Assessment** — evaluating learner understanding of concepts and their
  relationships.
* **Ontology building** — a first step in defining the ontology of a domain.

== Concept maps vs. other visualizations

[cols="1,3"]
|===
| Type | Characteristics

| **Concept map**
| Multiple hubs and clusters; labeled relationships; hierarchical or
  free-form; propositions as fundamental units.

| **Mind map**
| Single central concept; tree structure radiating outward; primarily
  associative; no explicit linking phrases.

| **Topic map**
| Developed for information management; focuses on semantic interoperability
  of data; originated for book indices.

| **Argument map**
| Formal representation of reasoning; shows premises, conclusions, and
  objections.

| **ER diagram**
| Entities, attributes, and relationships for database design; formal
  cardinality notation.

| **UML class diagram**
| Classes, attributes, operations, and relationships for object-oriented
  software design.
|===

== Guidelines

* Formulate a clear **focus question** before starting.
* Use **short labels** for concepts (1-4 words).
* Use **precise linking phrases** that form meaningful propositions when read
  with the connected concepts.
* Place the most **general concepts at the top** and more specific ones below.
* Add **cross-links** to show connections across different domains.
* Keep the map **simple enough** to be understood by its intended audience.
* **Revise and refine** the map as understanding evolves.
* Concept maps are often **personal and idiosyncratic** — there is no single
  "correct" map.
